The problem

It should not be all or nothing

Technology platforms today offer two settings: everything, or nothing.

You have a phone, and you are exposed to every scammer, every algorithm, every gambling operator, every predatory advertiser, every unsolicited contact that wants access to you. The platform was designed to maximise that exposure — it is not a side effect, it is the business model.

Or you don't have a phone. And you are digitally excluded — from your bank, from DVA, from your GP's patient portal, from your family, from the services that exist specifically to support you.

The platform is not a neutral environment that happens to contain bad actors. It is an environment deliberately engineered by some of the most sophisticated behavioural scientists in the world to maximise the time you spend in it — regardless of the cost to you. Infinite scroll. Notification alerts tuned to the same dopamine pathways as a poker machine. Algorithms that learn what provokes your strongest response and serve you more of it.

The digital reality

A neighbourhood with no streetlights, no closing hours, no regulated shopfronts — and every door permanently open to whoever wants to walk in. With strobing lights on every corner, free poker machines in every shopfront, and psychologists hired to make sure you never go home.

or
The only alternative

Never leave the house. Be digitally excluded. No DVA access. No online banking. No communication with family. No health services. Isolated from the support that exists specifically for you.

What we are building

The third option that does not currently exist. A designed digital neighbourhood — safe streets, regulated shopfronts, trusted community — where a veteran can be connected without being at constant risk.

The protection gap

We fix the neighbourhood.
Not the individual rooms.

The current approach to protecting vulnerable people online is a renovation strategy. Go to the bookmaker and ask them to add a warning. Ask the bank to flag suspicious transfers. Ask social media to label scam accounts. Each one is a well-meaning tweak to an individual room — but the vulnerable person still walks through the same unsafe streets to get there.

VetPhone is not asking industries to retrofit protections into environments never designed with veterans in mind. It is building the street those veterans live on — a trusted digital neighbourhood designed from the ground up around their specific circumstances, vulnerabilities, and needs.

"We are not playing the game on their terms. We are building our own neighbourhood."

What this is not

Not an IT project. The moment it becomes a technology solution, it gets handed to IT people and the veteran disappears from the conversation. The technology is infrastructure. It does not appear until page three.

Not a commercial product. The moment the focus becomes monetisation, the veteran becomes the product or the customer rather than the beneficiary.

Not a content filter. VetPhone does not attempt to make every app environment safe. Some digital environments — social media platforms, gambling apps — cannot be reformed through this method. They are simply not given a shopfront in this neighbourhood. The betting shop is legal. It does not have premises here.

The precedent this sets

If VetPhone works, it becomes a proof of concept for something larger. Politicians can point to it. Researchers can publish from it. Advocacy groups can hold it up and say: people chose this. They preferred it.

The "you can't fight human nature" argument that Big Tech relies on — that people want infinite scroll and dopamine alerts — becomes harder to make when a real cohort of real people demonstrably chose the alternative and were better for it.

This can inform what digital duty of care legislation looks like in practice, because it will have run the experiment. Not theoretically — in Hobart, with real veterans, with real data.

Why veterans

Concentrated wealth.
Documented vulnerability.

Australian veterans hold concentrated, identifiable, tax-free lump sum wealth — publicly known to be in the hundreds of thousands of dollars. They are disproportionately older, disproportionately isolated, and carry psychological conditions that compound digital vulnerability.

They are currently asked to navigate that exposure in a completely unstructured digital environment, with the burden on them individually. That is a systemic failure, not a personal one.

DVA compensation payments under the MRCA can reach $580,000 as a tax-free lump sum — and veterans are encouraged to seek financial advice on where to put it. That advice, and the platforms used to act on it, may come from anyone.

PTSD — documented in a significant proportion of the veteran community — creates a specific pattern of digital vulnerability: institutional trust that makes impersonation of DVA or government services highly effective, combined with social isolation that removes the informal check of a friend saying "that looks dodgy."

Australian research found veterans experience problem gambling at nearly twice the rate of the general population. The digital bookmaker is in their pocket, available every hour of every day, with no closing time and no bouncer.

The interface

Four screens. One designed experience.

Not separate tools requiring separate decisions. One calm environment, built around the person and their actual needs. Nothing designed to provoke anxiety or reward compulsive checking.

The VeteranHub screen shows what a veteran sees when they tap into that trusted source — a fully designed environment built around their community, in their language.

How we work

The principles that don't move

Every decision — design, partnership, funding, scope — is tested against these before it is made.

01

Veteran-led. Always.

Veterans at the Veterans Hub in Hobart are the co-designers, the testers, and the measure of whether this project succeeds. Every design decision starts with one question: does this work for the person holding the phone?

02

Not an IT project.

The moment this becomes a technology solution, it gets handed to IT people and the veteran disappears from the conversation. The technology is infrastructure — it serves the human outcome, it does not define it.

03

Not commercial-first.

If commercial partners come in, the structure requires them to serve the established veteran-centred framework — not the other way around. The veteran is the beneficiary, not the product or the customer.

04

Calm by design.

Every interface decision should reduce cognitive load, not increase it. The product should feel like relief. Nothing designed to provoke anxiety, compulsive checking, or engagement for its own sake.

The partnership

Five partners. One proof of concept.

Built in Tasmania, for Tasmania, with the ambition to demonstrate something nationally significant. Every partner has a defined role — and the veteran comes first.

A Tasmanian story

Built here.
For here.
Scalable from here.

This was not conceived in a Sydney startup incubator. It was not funded by a venture capital firm with an exit strategy. It started because someone who lives in Hobart, works in Hobart, and sees veterans walk into the Veterans Hub every day thought: this is not good enough, and I can help fix it.

Tasmania is the right scale for this proof of concept. Small enough that stakeholder relationships are personal and feedback loops are short. Large enough that results are meaningful, publishable, and generalisable.

UTAS brings research infrastructure. Veterans Hub brings the community. The Everyday Technologist brings the problem definition and the platform. A Tasmanian technology partner brings the build capability. Together, they can demonstrate something that changes the national conversation.

Tasmania has a habit of producing things that are small in origin and significant in effect.
